## Main argument

Reinforcement learning treats trading as sequential decision-making: an agent observes a state, chooses an action, receives a reward, and learns a policy intended to maximize long-run cumulative reward. The chapter moves from finite Markov decision processes and dynamic programming through Q-learning to deep Q-networks, then implements a simplified single-stock trading environment (PDF pp. 691-723).

The conceptual fit with trading is strong, but the empirical burden is higher than for supervised learning. The environment, reward, state, actions, transaction mechanics, and delayed credit assignment jointly define what the agent learns. A toy agent can optimize simulator artifacts or repeated historical paths rather than a durable trading policy (PDF pp. 692-696, 716-723).

## Concepts and definitions

- **Agent/environment:** the agent receives observations, chooses actions, and the environment returns a new state and scalar reward (PDF pp. 692-693).
- **Policy \(\pi\):** maps states to actions or action probabilities (PDF p. 693).
- **Reward:** immediate scalar feedback; reward design communicates the objective and can include P&L, cost, volatility, or drawdown (PDF pp. 693-694).
- **Value function:** expected long-run return from a state or state-action pair under a policy (PDF pp. 694, 697-699).
- **Model-based RL:** learns or uses transition/reward dynamics for planning.
- **Model-free RL:** learns directly by trial and error (PDF p. 694).
- **Credit assignment:** attributes delayed outcomes to earlier decisions.
- **Exploration/exploitation:** balances learning about uncertain actions against using the current best policy (PDF p. 695).
- **Markov decision process (MDP):** assumes the current state contains all information needed for the future transition distribution (PDF pp. 696-699).
- **Dynamic programming:** uses known transition probabilities; policy and value iteration solve finite MDPs (PDF pp. 699-706).
- **Temporal-difference learning:** bootstraps from intermediate value estimates rather than waiting for episode completion (PDF pp. 696, 707-708).
- **Q-learning:** learns a state-action value function from sampled rewards without known transition probabilities (PDF pp. 707-708).
- **DQN/DDQN:** neural approximation of \(Q\), experience replay, a target network, and decoupled action selection/value estimation for continuous state spaces with discrete actions (PDF pp. 709-714).

## Formulas and notation

Only equations verified visually or directly from displayed code are included.

- Discounted expected return:

  \[
  G_t
  =\mathbb{E}\!\left[R_{t+1}+\gamma R_{t+2}+\gamma^2R_{t+3}+\cdots\right]
  =\sum_{s=0}^{T}\gamma^s\mathbb{E}[R_{t+s}]
  \]

  and its recursive form:

  \[
  G_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma G_{t+1}
  \]

  where \(0\le\gamma\le1\) is the discount factor (PDF p. 697). The summation index is preserved as printed.

- State-value function:

  \[
  v_\pi(s)
  =\mathbb{E}_\pi[G_t\mid S_t=s]
  =\mathbb{E}_\pi\!\left[
  \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^kR_{t+k+1}
  \,\middle|\,s_t=s
  \right]
  \]

  (PDF p. 697).

- Value-iteration update:

  \[
  v_{k+1}(s)\leftarrow
  \max_a\sum_{s'}\sum_r
  p(s',r\mid s,a)\left[r+\gamma v_k(s')\right]
  \]

  (PDF p. 700).

- Tabular Q-learning update, as implemented by the chapter:

  \[
  Q(s,a)\leftarrow Q(s,a)+
  \alpha\left[
  r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a')-Q(s,a)
  \right]
  \]

  (PDF pp. 707-708).

- DQN target generated by the slow target network:

  \[
  y_i=
  \mathbb{E}\!\left[
  r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a';\theta^-)
  \mid s,a
  \right]
  \]

  (PDF p. 710).

## Assumptions

- The state is sufficiently Markovian; omitted history does not materially change transitions or rewards (PDF pp. 696-697).
- The simulator's transition and reward rules approximate the live market and execution process.
- Historical interactions provide enough coverage of relevant state-action pairs.
- The reward encodes the true objective and cannot be gamed through simulator artifacts (PDF pp. 693-695).
- Market dynamics are stable enough for bootstrapped value estimates to generalize.
- Randomly sampled replay transitions remain relevant while the policy and market distribution change.
- In the toy trading environment, the agent can switch among all-short, flat, and all-long positions at assumed costs without market impact (PDF pp. 716-720).

## Procedures described by the chapter

### Finite MDP

1. Define states, actions, rewards, transition probabilities, terminal states, and discount factor.
2. Initialize policy/value estimates.
3. Alternate policy evaluation and greedy policy improvement, or collapse them into value iteration.
4. Stop when values/policy converge (PDF pp. 696-706).

### Q-learning

1. Initialize \(Q(s,a)\).
2. Start each episode in a state.
3. Select a random action with probability \(\epsilon\), otherwise the current greedy action.
4. Observe reward and next state.
5. Apply the temporal-difference update.
6. Continue until terminal; repeat across episodes (PDF pp. 707-708).

### DQN/DDQN

1. Approximate action values with an online network.
2. Store `(state, action, reward, next_state, done)` transitions.
3. Sample replay minibatches.
4. Select next actions with the online network and evaluate them with a slower target network.
5. Fit the online network to TD targets.
6. Periodically copy online weights to the target network.
7. Decay \(\epsilon\) from exploration toward exploitation (PDF pp. 709-715).

### Trading environment

1. `DataSource` loads prices, computes returns and technical features, normalizes them, and emits observations.
2. `TradingSimulator` tracks position, trades, costs, strategy NAV, and a benchmark.
3. `TradingEnvironment` exposes Gym-style `reset()` and `step()` methods.
4. A DDQN repeatedly selects short/flat/long actions, stores transitions, replays experience, and is compared with buy-and-hold and random policies (PDF pp. 716-722).

## Feature and model examples

- Trading state: recent one-, two-, and five-period returns, volume, StochRSI, and ATR (PDF pp. 716-718).
- Actions: fully long, flat, or fully short (PDF p. 716).
- Reward: prior position times the latest market return minus trading/time costs; NAV endpoints terminate the episode (PDF pp. 716, 718-719).
- DQN refinements: prioritized replay, slow target network, and Double DQN (PDF pp. 710-714).
- Trading network: two 64-unit dense layers, dropout, and three outputs; 5,059 trainable parameters (PDF pp. 720-721).
- Training: 2,000 one-year episodes, roughly 500,000 steps, using AAPL history (PDF pp. 721-722).

## Statistical, validation, and backtesting warnings

- The environment is part of the model. Unrealistic costs, fills, state timing, episode resets, and terminal rules can create a policy that exploits the simulator.
- Rewards are endogenous to actions, observations are serially correlated, and their distribution changes as the policy changes (PDF pp. 695-696, 709).
- Delayed rewards make credit assignment difficult, especially for many positions or risk controls (PDF p. 695).
- Repeated random episodes from the same price history are not independent "years" of market evidence.
- The chapter explicitly warns that a single-stock agent has a very high overfitting risk and should be tested on new data (PDF p. 722).
- The benchmark is frictionless while the agent pays costs; useful for a high hurdle, but it does not repair training/test reuse.
- A reward based only on next-period return can encourage turnover, hidden tail risk, or unstable exposure unless costs and risk are complete.
- DQN bootstraps from its own changing estimates; feedback can diverge. Replay and target networks mitigate but do not eliminate this.

## Implementation patterns worth preserving

- Separate immutable market data, environment mechanics, portfolio accounting, agent logic, replay storage, and evaluation.
- Treat every observation and reward timestamp as an auditable state transition.
- Write synthetic environment tests for action-to-position mapping, cost timing, terminal handling, forced exits, and NAV reconciliation.
- Compare against deterministic no-trade, always-long, always-short, simple rule, random, and supervised-policy baselines.
- Save the environment config, code version, seeds, episode start dates, replay policy, network weights, and evaluation paths.
- Evaluate only on chronologically held-out real episodes and report repeated-seed dispersion.

## One-minute GC adaptation

RL should be deferred until a directional policy and execution contract are independently justified. If authorized later:

- State at decision time may contain only information through completed bar \(t\).
- Actions must reflect explicit flat/long/short/close/hold semantics and whether new entries are allowed.
- Entry occurs at the open of \(t+1\), not at bar-\(t\) close.
- Enforce London/New York entry windows, no entries at/after noon, same-date paths, and forced closure before 15:30 New York.
- Reset or handle contract/instrument/segment/roll boundaries exactly as the trusted population does.
- Include tick size, commission, slippage, spread assumptions, position limits, drawdown, and any prop-firm constraints in portfolio accounting.
- Reward should be net and risk-aware, while avoiding hand-shaped terms that encode the desired policy.
- Train only on Development; select once on Validation; evaluate real chronological Final-test episodes once after the environment and policy are frozen.
- Report performance against the frozen non-RL strategy, no-trade, always-long/short, and random policies across seeds and years.

## Unsuitable or deferred ideas

- Implementing RL in the first MLAT feature batch.
- Treating 2,000 resampled episodes as 2,000 independent years.
- Letting an agent observe same-bar high/low/close and transact earlier in that bar.
- Reusing Validation or Final-test episodes during exploration, replay, early stopping, or reward tuning.
- A reward that ignores transaction costs, risk, forced exits, or path-dependent loss limits.
- Training on one continuous GC history without regime/contract/session holdouts.
- Using RL performance as evidence that the underlying OHLCV features are predictive.
