## Main argument

Unsupervised learning can reveal structure without using an outcome label. The chapter develops two complementary tasks: dimensionality reduction constructs a smaller representation of the original features, while clustering groups similar observations or features. It then connects these methods to trading through data-driven risk factors, eigenportfolios, and hierarchical risk parity (HRP) (PDF pp. 429-430, 443-460).

The methods do not make discovery objective-free. PCA privileges variance, ICA privileges statistical independence, manifold methods privilege local-neighborhood structure, and clustering results depend on distance, scale, linkage, density, and other design choices. Those assumptions determine whether the discovered structure is economically meaningful or merely an artifact of representation (PDF pp. 431-442, 448-455).

## Concepts and definitions

- **Dimensionality reduction:** transforms the original features into fewer variables while attempting to preserve information relevant to the chosen criterion (PDF pp. 429-431).
- **Curse of dimensionality:** with a fixed number of observations, increasing the number of independent features makes the feature space sparse and typical distances larger, increasing the data required for reliable learning (PDF pp. 430-433).
- **Manifold hypothesis:** high-dimensional observations may lie on or near a lower-dimensional structure that can be represented more compactly (PDF pp. 430-431, 440-442).
- **Principal component analysis (PCA):** rotates data into mutually orthogonal directions that capture successively less variance. The components are uncorrelated linear combinations of the original features (PDF pp. 433-439).
- **Explained-variance ratio:** the share of total variance captured by a component; its cumulative curve can help choose a reduced dimension, although an elbow is a diagnostic rather than an objective truth (PDF pp. 434, 439, 444-445).
- **Independent component analysis (ICA):** estimates statistically independent, non-Gaussian source components from linear mixtures. Independence is stronger than PCA's lack of correlation (PDF pp. 439-440).
- **t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE):** a nonlinear visualization method that converts proximity into probabilities and emphasizes preserving local neighbors in the low-dimensional map (PDF pp. 441-442).
- **Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP):** a nonlinear embedding based on local connectedness and fuzzy topology; it is presented as faster than t-SNE and sometimes better at preserving global structure (PDF p. 442).
- **Eigenportfolio:** a portfolio whose weights are derived from a principal component of an asset-return correlation or covariance structure (PDF pp. 446-448).
- **Clustering:** assigns existing observations to similarity-based groups rather than constructing new features (PDF pp. 448-449).
- **k-means:** partitions observations around \(k\) centroids to minimize within-cluster variation, normally using Euclidean distance (PDF pp. 449-451).
- **Silhouette coefficient:** compares an observation's average distance within its assigned cluster with its distance to the nearest alternative cluster (PDF pp. 450-451).
- **Hierarchical clustering:** builds nested clusters through successive merges or splits. A dendrogram records the sequence and dissimilarity at which merges occur (PDF pp. 451-453).
- **DBSCAN and HDBSCAN:** density-based methods that can identify non-spherical clusters and leave noise unassigned; HDBSCAN searches across neighborhood radii to accommodate differing densities (PDF pp. 454-455).
- **Gaussian mixture model (GMM):** a probabilistic model that represents observations as generated by a mixture of multivariate normal distributions and produces soft membership probabilities (PDF p. 455).
- **Hierarchical risk parity (HRP):** clusters assets by their dependence structure, quasi-diagonalizes the correlation matrix, and recursively allocates weight between subclusters according to their relative variance (PDF pp. 455-458).

## Formulas and notation

Only formulas recoverable from the supplied PDF are included. The covariance and eigendecomposition equations on PDF p. 436, silhouette equation on PDF p. 450, and HRP allocation code on PDF p. 457 were checked against rendered pages.

- For observations \(k=1,\ldots,N\), the sample covariance between features \(i\) and \(j\) is:

  \[
  \operatorname{cov}_{i,j}
  =
  \frac{\sum_{k=1}^{N}(x_{ik}-\bar{x}_i)(x_{jk}-\bar{x}_j)}
       {N-1}
  \]

  (PDF pp. 435-436).

- An eigenvector \(w_i\) and eigenvalue \(\lambda_i\) of a square matrix \(M\) satisfy:

  \[
  Mw_i=\lambda_i w_i
  \]

  With eigenvectors as columns of \(W\) and eigenvalues on the diagonal of \(L\), the chapter writes:

  \[
  M=WLW^{-1}
  \]

  (PDF p. 436). For a symmetric covariance matrix, the eigenvectors supply the principal-component directions.

- ICA describes observed mixtures \(X\) as linear combinations of latent sources through an unknown mixing matrix \(A\), then seeks the inverse unmixing matrix \(W=A^{-1}\) to recover the sources. The chapter stresses that uniqueness depends on independent, non-Gaussian sources and an invertible \(A\) (PDF pp. 439-440).

- If \(a\) is an observation's mean distance to members of its assigned cluster and \(b\) is its mean distance to the nearest other cluster, the silhouette score is:

  \[
  s=\frac{b-a}{\max(a,b)}\in[-1,1]
  \]

  (PDF p. 450).

- HRP converts correlation \(\rho_{ij}\) to a bounded distance:

  \[
  d_{ij}=\sqrt{\frac{1-\rho_{ij}}{2}}
  \]

  (PDF p. 456).

- For covariance submatrix \(\Sigma_c\), the chapter first builds normalized inverse-variance weights \(w_i\propto 1/\Sigma_{ii}\), then measures cluster variance as:

  \[
  v_c=w^\mathsf{T}\Sigma_c w
  \]

  At each bisection, the allocation to the first subcluster is \(1-v_0/(v_0+v_1)\), with the complement assigned to the second (PDF p. 457).

## Assumptions

- PCA assumes that high variance is informative, feature scales are comparable, linear transformations capture the relevant structure, and first and second moments are sufficient. The chapter notes that the implied emphasis can be a poor fit for skewed or fat-tailed market data (PDF pp. 434-435).
- PCA and other distance-sensitive methods require an explicit missing-value, outlier, and scaling policy; results can change materially with those choices (PDF pp. 434, 443-447, 449-451).
- ICA assumes independent non-Gaussian sources, a linear mixing process, and an invertible mixing matrix; the data must be centered and whitened (PDF pp. 439-440).
- t-SNE and UMAP assume that neighborhood relationships are meaningful. A visually separated embedding is not itself evidence of stable clusters, labels, or future return predictability (PDF pp. 441-442).
- k-means assumes compact, roughly spherical, similarly sized clusters and ignores covariance among features (PDF pp. 449-450).
- Hierarchical clustering assumes that the chosen distance and linkage rule express the relevant similarity; early agglomerative merges cannot be reversed (PDF pp. 451-453).
- DBSCAN/HDBSCAN assume that density and neighborhood definitions can separate meaningful structure from noise, while GMM assumes a mixture of multivariate normal distributions (PDF pp. 454-455).
- HRP assumes that a historically estimated dependence hierarchy is sufficiently stable to guide future allocation and that recursively treating nearby assets as substitutes is appropriate (PDF pp. 455-458).

## Procedures described by the chapter

### PCA and latent-factor workflow

1. Define observations and features, remove or impute missing values, control outliers, and standardize when feature scales differ.
2. Center the data and compute PCA through covariance eigendecomposition or singular value decomposition (SVD).
3. Examine individual and cumulative explained variance.
4. Select a component count using a predeclared criterion rather than retrospective visual preference alone.
5. Transform the observations into component scores.
6. Interpret loadings and stability before treating components as risk factors or portfolio weights (PDF pp. 433-448).

### Clustering workflow

1. Choose the objects to cluster, their feature representation, scale, and distance metric.
2. Choose an algorithm whose geometry matches the intended structure: centroid-, hierarchy-, density-, or probability-based.
3. Vary declared hyperparameters or linkage rules.
4. Assess internal quality with inertia, silhouettes, cophenetic correlation, or an application-aligned measure.
5. Refit on subsets or time blocks to test whether the grouping recurs.
6. Interpret clusters using information not consumed by the algorithm, without retroactively converting a visualization into a predictive claim (PDF pp. 448-455).

### HRP workflow and book backtest

1. Convert the asset-return correlation matrix to a distance matrix.
2. Apply hierarchical clustering and reorder assets so related series are adjacent.
3. Compute inverse-variance risk for each recursively bisected subcluster.
4. Allocate weight top-down, giving less weight to the higher-variance branch at each split.
5. In the chapter's example, ensemble ten gradient-boosting models, select the 25 stocks with the highest positive next-day forecasts, and rebalance long-only positions.
6. Compare HRP with equal weighting and mean-variance optimization using the same 2015-2017 US-equity strategy (PDF pp. 455-460).

## Feature and model examples

- PCA is demonstrated on a synthetic three-dimensional ellipse using covariance eigendecomposition, SVD, and `sklearn.decomposition.PCA` (PDF pp. 435-439).
- t-SNE and UMAP visualize Fashion-MNIST images across several perplexity or neighbor settings, showing that the map changes materially with the hyperparameter (PDF pp. 441-442).
- The latent-factor example starts with daily returns for large US equities in 2010-2018, winsorizes tails, applies coverage thresholds, and imputes remaining values with the daily cross-sectional mean. In the reported sample, the first component explains about 55 percent of daily return variation and roughly ten components explain 60 percent (PDF pp. 443-445).
- The eigenportfolio example normalizes returns, estimates components of the dependence matrix for 23 large stocks, and compares four component-weighted portfolios with equal weighting (PDF pp. 446-448).
- The clustering section compares k-means, several hierarchical linkage rules, DBSCAN/HDBSCAN, and GMM, emphasizing that each recognizes a different cluster geometry (PDF pp. 448-455).
- The HRP example trades up to 25 positively forecast US stocks and computes weights from one year of daily price history (PDF pp. 458-459).

## Statistical, validation, and backtesting warnings

- “Unsupervised” does not mean leakage-free. Fitting scalers, winsorization limits, PCA loadings, embeddings, clusters, or covariance hierarchies on future observations lets future distributional information influence earlier representations.
- Component signs are arbitrary, and components can rotate or reorder when eigenvalues are close. A component number is not a persistent economic identity without alignment and stability checks (PDF pp. 433-439).
- Maximizing explained variance does not maximize predictive information, economic utility, or robustness. Low-variance directions may contain signal, while a dominant component may only capture broad volatility (PDF pp. 434-445).
- t-SNE does not provide a natural transform for new observations in the implementation described and distorts large versus small distances differently. Its map should not be passed mechanically to a clustering algorithm or read as a forecast (PDF pp. 441-442).
- k-means reaches a local optimum and depends on initialization, \(k\), scaling, outliers, and assumed spherical geometry (PDF pp. 449-451).
- Dendrogram height and appearance are linkage-dependent; the chapter explicitly warns against comparing the “look” of dendrograms across linkage methods (PDF pp. 452-453).
- Density clustering can be unstable under neighborhood settings and varying density; GMM flexibility does not validate its distributional assumptions (PDF pp. 454-455).
- The chapter's HRP comparison is one strategy, universe, period, and set of portfolio choices. Mean-variance optimization beats HRP in the reported test, and the chapter states that the ranking is sensitive to the number of stocks, period, and other factors (PDF pp. 458-460).
- Selecting an embedding, cluster count, linkage, component count, or allocator after viewing downstream performance is model selection and must be recorded as such.

## Implementation patterns worth preserving

- Represent preprocessing and unsupervised estimators as fitted artifacts with training-window dates, configuration, feature order, software versions, and hashes.
- Fit every learned transformation inside its Development fold, then call only `transform` or a declared assignment rule on later rows.
- Keep the raw causal feature table immutable; store components or cluster assignments as derived columns with provenance.
- Save PCA means, scales, loadings, eigenvalues, explained-variance ratios, and component-alignment diagnostics.
- For clusters, save the feature scale, distance metric, algorithm, initialization/seed, hyperparameters, labels, centroids or hierarchy, and stability metrics.
- Separate visualization embeddings from production representations.
- Compare unsupervised features on identical rows and folds against the unchanged baseline; do not allow representation fitting to alter eligibility or target construction.
- For covariance-based allocation, record estimation window, return frequency, missing-data treatment, covariance estimator, linkage, rebalance timing, and turnover/cost assumptions.

## Project 1 relevance

- PCA may be useful as a bounded redundancy-control challenger for correlated, causal one-minute GC features. It does not establish a trading signal by itself.
- Clustering may support exploratory state discovery or stability diagnostics, but a cluster label becomes a model-derived feature and must follow the same Development-only fitting and Validation governance as supervised models.
- Project 1 contains one GC instrument, so the chapter's cross-sectional latent risk factors, eigenportfolios, and HRP asset allocation do not transfer directly.
- The most transferable lesson is representation governance: use only information available by decision bar \(t\), learn transformations within chronological Development folds, freeze them before Validation, and compare them on the same eligible observations and targets.
- Given the existing anchor and ridge benchmarks, an unsupervised representation should earn inclusion by stable incremental Validation value, not by a compelling two-dimensional plot.

## One-minute GC adaptation

Potential bounded hypothesis:

> A small number of fold-fitted PCA scores, derived from a predeclared set of correlated causal GC state features, may preserve useful information while reducing estimation variance relative to the full feature block.

Adaptation requirements:

- Use only completed decision-bar information at \(t\); retain theoretical entry at the open of \(t+1\).
- Freeze the raw feature set before PCA. Winsorization, imputation, scaling, PCA fitting, and component-count selection must occur within each Development training fold.
- Choose the component count by a predeclared Development-only rule, and log every threshold or alternative as a research trial.
- Align component signs—and, when necessary, near-degenerate subspaces—using only training-window references before comparing loadings across folds.
- Evaluate London and New York sessions separately unless pooling and session interactions are predeclared.
- Compare raw-feature, PCA-feature, and anchor/ridge baselines on identical eligible rows, labels, horizons, folds, and losses.
- Test temporal stability of loadings and component distributions. Do not infer “regimes” from a t-SNE or UMAP plot.
- If clustering is studied later, define an out-of-sample assignment mechanism and frozen interpretation before Validation; exploratory clusters alone are not deployable states.
